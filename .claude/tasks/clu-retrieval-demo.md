## The question
The program's thesis is that CLU is an **addressable dynamical memory**: items are written into the learned landscape, and retrieved by **launching a particle of mass `m` from initial latent `(q₀,p₀)` and reading its trajectory**. **Nobody has ever demonstrated this loop — not on a task, not on a toy.** Before we design learned encoders, address selectors and readouts, we should know whether the *physics* supports selective storage and retrieval at all.

**So: hand-build it. No learning in stage 1.** Hand-design a potential holding ≥2 distinguishable items, hand-pick the addresses, and measure whether retrieval is selective, durable, and readable.

## Items
1. ⭐ **The minimal demo: two items, two addresses, selective retrieval.**
   - Construct a `V_θ` (hand-designed, e.g. two wells / two coset registers / a ring with two marked sectors) holding **two distinguishable stored items**.
   - Define two addresses `(m₁,q₀₁,p₀₁)` and `(m₂,q₀₂,p₀₂)`.
   - Roll out from each. **Measure: does address 1 retrieve item 1 and not item 2?** Quantify selectivity — e.g. a linear probe on the trajectory recovering the item identity, and a confusion matrix. **A linear read is the bar** (CAFE's contract is a frozen embedding + linear probe; a retrieval needing a nonlinear decoder is much weaker evidence).
   - **Report retrieval accuracy, and the trajectory length over which it survives.** Use `rollout_diag.py` for the spread/collapse side.
2. ⭐ **Does MASS actually work as an address key?** This is the vision's load-bearing claim and the least-tested. Hold `(q₀,p₀)` **fixed** and vary **only `m`** — do different masses retrieve different items from the same launch point? **If mass alone cannot discriminate, the "choose the appropriate mass particle" step fails and the vision needs a different key.** Report plainly either way. *(Context: Hub verified masses are effectively frozen in all training runs — `mass_lr_mult=1.0`, unreferenced in `clu_scorer.py` — so mass has never been exercised as a functional lever in this program.)*
3. **The three write modes — can you realize them side by side?** In one landscape: (a) a **permanent** item (flat/latch direction, `μ²≈0`); (b) a **decaying** item written *near* (a) with a finite `μ²`, so it has a half-life; (c) an **uncorrelated** item at a fresh location (broken-symmetry points or a stable trajectory). **Measure the half-life of (b) and check it does not corrupt (a)** — write locality is a known weak point (an MLP potential has no locality by default; you may need a designed/structured potential, which is fine and should be stated).
4. **Interference scaling.** Add items one at a time (2 → 4 → 8 …) and measure when retrieval degrades. **Report the number of items at which selectivity drops below a stated threshold.** ⚠ Realism check: measured register capacity is **1–1.6 bits** (v5-gate). **If the practical ceiling is a handful of items, say so bluntly — it is decisive for the "transformer competitor" framing and is much better learned now than after a wave of architecture work.**
5. ⭐ **Probe the learnability crux — in its WEAK form (Head's 2026-07-21 amendment).** The Head has clarified that memory learning is **iterative and gauge-loose**, not one-shot or precise: *"encode to an arbitrary region → see how it fairs on the training loss → restructure over epochs"*, and *"allow arbitrary choices for location of a register or masses if these parameters don't affect performance much."* ⇒ **The question is NOT "can we recover the exact address by gradient" but "can a DELIBERATELY BAD initial address be restructured into a working one by descending a retrieval loss?"** Two measurements, in this order:
   - **(a) The restructuring test (primary).** Start from a deliberately wrong `(m, q₀, p₀)` — one that retrieves the wrong item or nothing — define a simple retrieval loss, and **run plain gradient descent on the address for a modest number of steps. Does it find a working address?** Report the success rate over several bad initializations and how many steps it takes. **This is the single most informative number in the task**, because it tests the vision's actual learning story rather than a stronger one nobody is claiming.
   - **(b) Smoothness (secondary, cheap).** Perturb `q₀` and see whether retrieval quality varies smoothly or fractally; a gradient-norm-vs-rollout-length curve if it is easy. The worry is that conservative dynamics do not contract, so `q₀`-perturbations never shrink and long-rollout gradients may be pathological. **(a) can succeed even if (b) looks ugly** — report both and do not let (b)'s appearance override (a)'s result.
   **Timebox this; it is a measurement, not an optimization project.**
6. **PREREG before running** (`PREREG.md`). Predict: selectivity at 2 items; whether mass alone discriminates; the item count at which interference bites; whether the `q₀` loss surface is smooth. **You have falsified Hub predictions twice and both times it was the most valuable part of the wave — do it again if the data says so.**
